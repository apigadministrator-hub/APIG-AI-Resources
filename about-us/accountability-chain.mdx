This site is built around a single idea: no public employee, official, or agency operates in isolation. Every action, condition, decision, or failure at the ground level connects upward through a chain of supervision, authority, funding, and legal responsibility, all the way to the State of Illinois. The Accountability Project follows that chain so citizens and journalists can see the full picture, not just the bottom rung.

## The Starting Point Is Never the Endpoint

When something goes wrong in government, the public is often told a single low-level worker is responsible. The Accountability Project starts with that worker, but does not stop there. The worker is the starting point for tracing responsibility upward, not the endpoint.

Here is a concrete example. A groundskeeper at a county housing authority cemetery notices that a grave marker is damaged and does not report it. The Accountability Project asks not just "did the groundskeeper do their job?" but:

- Who trained this worker?
- Who supervises them?
- What policy governs maintenance inspections?
- Who approved that policy?
- Who funds this authority?
- Who appointed the board?
- What does Illinois law require?

Each of those questions points to another link in the chain. That is how this site is organized, and that is how citizens can understand what really happened.

## The Responsibility Ladder

<Steps>
  <Step title="The Worker or Frontline Employee">
    The person who performed the act, was present, or has direct knowledge. They may be the source of physical evidence and direct facts. But they are rarely the only responsible party.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The Supervisor">
    The person who directed, managed, trained, or scheduled the worker. Supervisors are responsible for what they knew, should have known, directed, allowed, or failed to prevent.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The Department, Office, or Operating Authority">
    The organizational unit responsible for staffing, budgets, equipment, contracts, and written policies. Failures here often involve inadequate resources, missing procedures, or poor record-keeping.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The Appointing and Administrative Authority">
    The mayor, county executive, township trustee, or county board chair who appointed or oversees department leadership. Responsible for setting expectations, evaluating performance, and acting on complaints.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The Governing Board">
    The elected or appointed body with ultimate oversight authority. Responsible for approving budgets, setting policy, receiving audits, and ensuring compliance. Ignorance of known problems is not a defense.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The State of Illinois">
    Where state law, state funding, state licensing, state regulation, or a state agency is involved, Illinois is part of the accountability chain. This includes HUD-funded housing authorities (federal), IDPH-regulated facilities, DCFS-involved agencies, state grants, and any authority created under Illinois statute.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## What Cannot Be Delegated Away

Some responsibilities cannot be transferred to a subordinate. A governing board cannot delegate its duty of oversight. A county executive cannot delegate ultimate responsibility for a department's conduct merely by appointing a department head. Each level retains specific duties that stay with it no matter who else is involved.

| Level | Non-Delegable Duty |
| --- | --- |
| Worker | Duty to report observed violations, hazards, or failures to the supervisor |
| Supervisor | Duty to know what subordinates are doing, to train them, and to act on reports |
| Department / Authority | Duty to maintain written policies, adequate staffing, and accurate records |
| Appointing Authority | Duty to evaluate leadership performance and act on documented failures |
| Governing Board | Duty of oversight: approve budgets, receive audits, ensure compliance |
| State of Illinois | Duty to enforce state law, inspect regulated entities, and respond to violations |

## The Integrity Meter

Each entity profile on this site includes a public integrity meter. Visitors can click thumbs up or thumbs down to signal their perception of that entity's integrity and accountability. This is not a vote or legal finding. It is a public signal, a way for residents, journalists, and affected people to express whether they believe the entity is meeting its public responsibilities. The meter is displayed publicly and permanently on the profile. All signals are anonymous. Results are cumulative.

## Records That Establish Responsibility

Public records are the evidence that connects each link in the chain. Here are the record types that establish responsibility at each level, what they show, and where to find them.

| Record Type | What It Shows | Where to Find It |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meeting minutes | Board decisions, knowledge of issues, votes | FOIA request / public website |
| Employment records | Who was hired, pay, training | FOIA request |
| Contracts and bids | Who was paid, for what, on what terms | FOIA request |
| Inspection reports | Compliance status, known deficiencies | State agency, FOIA |
| Budget documents | What was funded, what was cut | Public records / FOIA |
| Policy manuals | What the rules required | FOIA request |
| Incident reports | What was reported, to whom, when | FOIA request |
| Correspondence | What officials knew and when | FOIA request |

## Questions to Ask at Each Level

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Worker / Frontline">
    What did you see, do, or report? Were you trained? Did you have the equipment and time to do the job properly?
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Supervisor">
    Were you aware? When did you know? What did you do? What records exist?
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Department / Authority">
    What is the written policy? Who approved it? When was the last inspection or audit? What were the results?
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Appointing Authority">
    Were you informed? What corrective action did you take? What performance evaluations exist?
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Governing Board">
    What did the board know? What was reported at meetings? Was this in the budget? Was an audit conducted?
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="State of Illinois">
    What does state law require? Is the entity in compliance? Has the state inspected or audited? What is the enforcement history?
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## How to Follow the Chain on This Site

Every entity page on the Accountability Project includes a clickable breadcrumb at the top. That breadcrumb links upward to the entity's supervisor, governing authority, and the State of Illinois. Readers can click from a maintenance worker all the way up to Illinois and back down. Every entity has its own accountability chain clearly displayed, so you can see the full path of responsibility in a single view.

<Note>
  The Accountability Project does not determine legal liability. It documents public responsibility. Legal findings are for courts. Public accountability is for the public.
</Note>
