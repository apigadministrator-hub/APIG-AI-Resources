The Accountability Project builds its records from a wide range of public and published material. This page explains what kinds of sources we use, how we distinguish between them, and how we handle situations where sources conflict or information changes.

## Types of Sources

We draw on several categories of source material:

- **Government websites:** Official portals, agency pages, elected official directories, and published reports.
- **Meeting minutes and agendas:** Records of public body meetings, including boards, councils, and commissions.
- **FOIA responses:** Documents obtained through the Illinois Freedom of Information Act and comparable federal and local processes.
- **Court records:** Dockets, filings, orders, and judgments from publicly accessible court systems.
- **Public filings:** Campaign finance reports, ethics disclosures, lobbyist registrations, and other mandatory filings.
- **News coverage:** Reporting from established news organizations, used for leads and context, but not treated as primary verification.

## Primary and Secondary Sources

A primary source is an original document or record created by the entity being documented: a meeting minute, a signed ordinance, a FOIA response, a court filing. A secondary source is a report, summary, or interpretation of that material by another party.

We prioritize primary sources. When a secondary source is used, we identify it as such and, where possible, seek the underlying primary document.

## Corroboration

We corroborate significant claims using independent sources. Two outlets repeating the same unverified claim does not constitute corroboration. True corroboration requires separate lines of evidence pointing to the same fact from different origins.

## Conflicting Information

When sources disagree, we do not pick a winner. Both accounts are preserved, the conflict is noted, and the record is marked as disputed. Users can see what each source says and evaluate the disagreement for themselves. Our role is to document the conflict, not resolve it by fiat.

## Freedom of Information Act

APIG uses FOIA requests extensively to obtain records that are not published proactively. We document each request: what was asked for, when it was submitted, and what was received. FOIA is a legal right, and using it systematically is part of our mission to make public information accessible.

## Corrections and Transparency

When an error is identified, we correct the record. The original version is preserved for transparency, so users can see what changed and when. Corrections are not hidden; they are part of the historical record.
